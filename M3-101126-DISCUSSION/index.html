When we speak

Position · Reason · Evidence · Position again

“Peace, you’re always stuffing yourself with burgers. You really shouldn’t, because they contain so much unhealthy fat and sugar. Did you know that one Big Mac — one! — contains 524 calories, which is a quarter of your calories for the day? And it also contains 8.8 g of sugar, which is more than a third of the sugar you’re supposed to eat in a single day. You always say you’re freaked out about diabetes — well, keep up with the Big Macs, burger boy, and your blood will be pure sugar. Stop complaining — you know I’m right. You’ve got to get off those burgers, my friend.”

Three ways to echo

1

Repeat their key words. “Hmmm. Two Big Macs.”

2

Say it back in your own words. “So you’re saying one burger is a fat and sugar bomb?”

3

Ask one question about it. “And what did that do to your weight?”

Do this before you disagree with anything.

Testing arguments

We don’t have to accept everything we hear.

Testing arguments

Three ways to challenge

1

Ask for the source. “Who says?” · “Where did that come from?”

2

Give a counter-example. “My cousin eats there every week and he’s fine.”

3

Say what the study missed. “That survey only asked adults.”

Challenge the evidence, not the person.

Conceding and rebuttal

Don’t reject everything. If the evidence is good, accept it.

Conceding

Three steps

1

Say their evidence back, with its source. “You said a Big Mac is 524 calories.”

2

Accept the part that is true. “That’s a quarter of my day. I accept that.”

3

Then give your own evidence. “But it still costs less than cooking for four.”

You cannot concede to a feeling. You can always concede to a number.

Narrowing and validating

Find the smaller thing you both agree on.

Narrowing

Three steps

1

Say what you both accept. “We both agree the food is not healthy.”

2

Name the smaller problem. “So the problem is how often, not the burger.”

3

Ask if they can accept it. “Can we agree on once a week?”

A smaller agreement is a real agreement.
